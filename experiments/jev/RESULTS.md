# System One, locally — results (phase 1)

Run 2026-09-19, deferral pass 2026-09-20. **Zero cloud calls — Jev itself is NOT measured.**
This characterises the local baseline a Jev comparison would have to beat. n=300 test items per
suite, single seed, paired bootstrap (2000 resamples) on every calibration and deferral claim.
CPU arms never touched the prod GPUs; the 27B arm is the live prod lane (`:8041`), at
~30 ms/decision and negligible load.

**The headline is Finding 8:** a 4B doing one forward pass on CPU, answering its confident 80%
and deferring the rest to the 27B, is statistically indistinguishable from the 27B alone
(0.904 vs 0.912 pooled, delta -0.007 [-0.021,+0.006]). Findings 1-5 are accuracy and
calibration; 6-8 are deferral, which is the property that decides whether any of this ships.
Reproduce with `python defer.py` and `python calib_transfer.py` -> `results/{deferral,transfer}.{txt,json}` (all committed; the
per-item `.jsonl` they read are regenerated by `prep_data.py` + `run_all.sh`, since the repo
does not track `.jsonl`).

## Setup

Four suites mirroring Jev's own demos: `injection` (2-way), `sentiment` (2), `routing` (11,
bitext support queues), `langid` (20). Disjoint seeded splits: `test` (scored), `calib` (fits
temperature only, never scored), `train` (embed-head only).

**Readout:** one forward pass, `max_tokens=1`, thinking off. The next-token distribution is
restricted to each option's first token and renormalised. First tokens are unique in every suite
(verified), so under a grammar permitting only the option strings, P(first token) **is exactly**
P(option) — this is not an approximation of constrained decoding, it is constrained decoding.
Every arm reads the **raw** distribution the same way, so arms are directly comparable.

## Finding 1 — letter-indexed options are hostile to small models

Same model, same items; only the answer format changes:

```
model          suite     letter    name
Qwen3.5-2B     langid     0.100   0.503
Qwen3.5-2B     routing    0.367   0.637
Qwen3.5-4B     langid     0.570   0.953
Qwen3.5-4B     injection  0.723   0.833
smart 27B      langid     0.873   0.993
```

Free generation confirmed this is the model, not the harness: the 2B answers "B" (Bulgarian) for
Polish and Spanish text. It knows the language; it cannot do the language-to-letter indirection
across 20 options. **The MMLU-style letter format is the wrong default for decision workloads.**
Emitting the typed choice directly — Jev's shape — is worth more than a model-size step.

## Finding 2 — post-hoc calibration does not transfer across tasks

`T-in` = temperature fitted on the same task's calib split. `T-loso` = fitted on the *other three*
tasks and applied to this one — the honest proxy for "calibrated on a task it has never seen".

```
condition                     sig. better   sig. WORSE   n.s.     (20 cells, 95% paired CI)
T-in   (same-task labels)               9            1     10
T-loso (transferred)                    6            5      9
```

Transfer is a coin flip, and where it hurts it hurts a lot:

```
arm              suite      ECE raw   ECE T-loso   delta [95% CI]
Qwen3.5-0.8B     routing      0.108       0.374    +0.252 [+0.173,+0.330]
Qwen3.5-4B       langid       0.037       0.206    +0.166 [+0.130,+0.196]
smart 27B        injection    0.087       0.150    +0.061 [+0.048,+0.068]
```

The reason is visible in the fitted temperatures: **tasks disagree about which direction is
wrong.** The 27B is under-confident on injection (T=0.43, needs sharpening) and over-confident on
routing (T=1.78, needs softening). No single global correction fixes both. This is precisely the
gap RLCD claims to close — and the first concrete, testable thing a Jev comparison should check.

## Finding 3 — the big model is already calibrated; the small ones are not

In-domain temperature does **not** significantly improve the 27B on any of the four suites (and
slightly worsens langid, +0.009). Its raw probabilities are already about as calibrated as
post-hoc fitting can make them: raw ECE 0.015-0.114. The small models are where calibration work
pays — e.g. 2B injection -0.088 [-0.113,-0.052], 0.8B sentiment -0.085 [-0.125,-0.035].

## Finding 4 — with labels, boring wins

A 0.6B embedder plus a softmax head beats the 27B zero-shot on **routing: 0.960 vs 0.803**.
Routing categories are dataset conventions; conventions can't be guessed. Its raw probabilities
are badly under-confident (my L2 choice), but in-domain temperature takes routing ECE 0.368 ->
0.021. If you have a few hundred labels, the zero-shot story has to justify itself on
"no labels needed", not on quality.

## Finding 5 — calibration alone is a trap metric

Qwen3.5-2B-letter on langid reaches ECE **0.006** after temperature scaling — "near-perfectly
calibrated" — at **10% accuracy**, by flattening to near-uniform (T=8.76). Calibrated and useless.
Deferral quality (AURC, selective accuracy) must be reported beside ECE, never instead of it.

## Finding 6 — deferral is where the arms actually separate, and it is not a restatement of accuracy

Risk-coverage, raw probabilities, name format. `sel@C` = accuracy on the C% of items the arm is
most confident about; AURC = mean error across all coverages, lower better, 2000-resample CI.
Full 32-cell table (both formats) in `results/deferral.txt`.

```
arm                suite       acc   sel@95  sel@90  sel@80  sel@50    AURC [95% CI]
smart 27B          injection  0.893   0.909   0.933   0.975   0.987   0.020 [0.010,0.033]
smart 27B          sentiment  0.957   0.968   0.978   0.983   0.980   0.016 [0.003,0.035]
smart 27B          routing    0.803   0.818   0.830   0.858   0.887   0.107 [0.072,0.151]
smart 27B          langid     0.993   1.000   1.000   1.000   1.000   0.000 [0.000,0.000]
Qwen3.5-4B         injection  0.833   0.846   0.859   0.896   0.960   0.063 [0.037,0.095]
Qwen3.5-4B         sentiment  0.947   0.961   0.967   0.979   1.000   0.010 [0.004,0.017]
Qwen3.5-4B         routing    0.730   0.758   0.781   0.808   0.847   0.162 [0.112,0.217]
Qwen3.5-4B         langid     0.953   0.965   0.970   0.979   0.993   0.011 [0.003,0.021]
Qwen3.5-2B         routing    0.637   0.660   0.681   0.696   0.813   0.179 [0.137,0.226]
Qwen3.5-2B         langid     0.503   0.523   0.548   0.592   0.800   0.226 [0.180,0.282]
embed-head 0.6B    routing    0.960   0.979   0.993   1.000   1.000   0.002 [0.001,0.005]
embed-head 0.6B    langid     0.720   0.744   0.778   0.804   0.967   0.087 [0.062,0.117]
```

Two things accuracy alone hides:

**The 27B's langid confidence is perfect, not just good.** AURC 0.0001, sel@95 = 1.000: its two
errors in 300 are both items it flagged as its least confident. That is the property a router
wants, and it is invisible in "0.993 accuracy".

**The 4B out-ranks its own accuracy.** On sentiment it is 0.947 raw but 1.000 at 50% coverage —
its errors are concentrated exactly where it is unsure. On langid, 0.953 raw, AURC 0.011,
statistically indistinguishable from the 27B's ranking quality on a suite where it gives up 4
points of accuracy.

**And the reverse failure exists.** `Qwen3.5-2B-letter` on langid has AURC **0.921** with
sel@50 = **0.080** — *below* its 0.100 overall accuracy. Its confidence is anti-correlated with
correctness: deferring its least-confident half makes the kept half worse. This is the same arm
that Finding 5 showed reaching ECE 0.006. Perfectly calibrated, actively misleading as a router.
Deferral cannot rescue an arm below the capability floor; it can only be poisoned by one.

## Finding 7 — temperature scaling cannot change a binary decision's deferral ordering. At all.

Every `k=2` cell in the dAURC table is exactly `+0.0000 [+0.0000,+0.0000]`. That is not a null
result, it is an identity. For two options, `max softmax = sigma(|z1 - z2| / T)`, which is
monotone increasing in `|z1 - z2|` for every `T > 0`. Temperature rescales the probability axis
but **cannot reorder items**, so AURC and selective-accuracy-at-fixed-coverage are invariant.

**Consequence for Jev's `noul` primitive:** post-hoc calibration on a boolean decision moves ECE
and Brier and cannot change which items a rank-based router hands off. It is not useless — it
sets *which probability value* corresponds to a given coverage, which is what you need to pick an
operating threshold offline. But the quality of the frontier is fixed at decode time. Any
"we calibrated it and routing improved" claim on a binary task needs a mechanism other than
temperature.

Where temperature *can* reorder (`k > 2`, 16 cells), it is a coin flip:

```
condition          better   WORSE   n.s.
T-in                    5       5      6
T-loso                  5       6      5
```

Same shape as Finding 2 and for the same reason. Calibration fitted in-domain reliably improves
*the numbers* (9 of 20 cells in Finding 2); it does not reliably improve *the decisions*.

## Finding 8 — a 4B on CPU with a 20% deferral budget is indistinguishable from the 27B

The production question, simulated exactly: every arm answered the same id-aligned test items, so
we can route item-by-item. The arm answers what it is confident about and hands the rest to the
`:8041` 27B lane. `defer%` is the share of items that cost a 27B call. Pooled over the four
suites (equal n), paired bootstrap resampling within suite:

```
arm                defer%   pooled acc   delta vs 27B alone      verdict
smart 27B alone      100%        0.912   --
Qwen3.5-4B             0%        0.866   -0.046 [-0.065,-0.027]  worse
Qwen3.5-4B            10%        0.887   -0.024 [-0.040,-0.008]  worse
Qwen3.5-4B            20%        0.904   -0.007 [-0.021,+0.006]  n.s.
Qwen3.5-4B            30%        0.917   +0.004 [-0.007,+0.015]  n.s.
embed-head 0.6B       30%        0.917   +0.007 [-0.011,+0.025]  n.s.
embed-head 0.6B       50%        0.938   +0.026 [+0.014,+0.038]  BEATS 27B
Qwen3.5-2B            50%        0.883   -0.031 [-0.046,-0.018]  worse
```

**80% of these decisions do not need a 27B.** A 4B running one forward pass, no chain of thought,
handling its confident 80% and deferring the rest, is within noise of the frontier lane on the
same items. The claim is "indistinguishable", not "beats" — the interval at 30% includes zero in
both directions and we are not calling +0.004 a win.

Per suite the budget varies a lot, and that is the useful detail (smallest deferral rate reaching
27B-alone accuracy, 1% grid):

```
arm              injection  sentiment  routing  langid
Qwen3.5-4B             19%        11%      31%     35%
Qwen3.5-2B             35%        80%      71%     90%
embed-head 0.6B         2%        47%       0%     56%
Qwen3.5-0.8B           69%        70%      47%    never
```

Sentiment needs 11% of a 27B; langid needs 35%; routing needs 31% from a 4B and **0%** from a
0.6B embedder with labels. There is no single "how much can the small model take" number — it is
a per-task property, and the calib split is enough to measure it.

### The threshold is deployable, not an oracle

Sections 3-4 defer a fixed *fraction* of test items, which quietly uses the test set to locate
the cut. The deployable version fits one confidence threshold on the **calib** split and applies
it unchanged: for the 4B, accuracy matches the oracle version to within ±0.010 in every cell and
exactly 0.000 in 7 of 12. What does *not* transfer cleanly is the **budget**: asking for 30%
deferral bought 27% on injection and 37% on langid. Plan the cost envelope with slack; the
accuracy is the stable part, the spend is not.

## Rung 0 — is the hard half SCALE or RANKING? (2026-09-20, zero new inference)

Finding 7 split a probability's job in two: the **ordering** of items, fixed at decode time, and
the **scale** that maps confidence to a thresholdable number. Only the second is post-hoc
reachable. `calib_transfer.py` asks how much is even on the table, using the runs we already had.

## Finding 9 — on any arm you would deploy, the entire scale budget is ~16% of its Brier

Isotonic fitted **on the test set itself** — an oracle, not a method — is the best *any* monotone
recalibration could ever do. `budget = raw − oracle` is therefore the whole scale term; what is
left is ranking-bound and only training moves it.

```
arms                              Brier   oracle floor   scale budget      worst cell
27B + 4B (name readout)           0.086          0.072   0.013 (16%)            0.030
  27B alone                       0.070          0.059   0.010 (15%)            0.021
  4B alone                        0.102          0.086   0.016 (16%)            0.030
0.8B / 2B-letter / embed-head     0.265          0.119   0.146 (55%)            0.494
```

**A perfect calibrator buys the 27B 0.010 Brier and the 4B 0.016.** Five-sixths of the error is
in the floor. The arms with a large scale budget are exactly the broken ones — the 0.8B below the
capability floor (0.443 on injection), the flattened 2B-letter (0.494 on langid), the
under-confident embed-head (0.400 on langid). **A big calibration budget is a symptom of a broken
arm, not an opportunity.**

This is the answer to "which half is hard", and it is not the convenient one: for a working
decision arm the hard half is **ranking**, and ranking is a training lever.

## Finding 10 — conditional temperature rescues broken arms and damages good ones

The cheap shortcut, tested properly: `log T = w · [entropy, margin, label_mass, log k, log len]`
— decode-time features only, no labels at inference — fitted on three suites' calib splits and
applied to a suite it has never seen, against the same fold's global scalar. A per-item
temperature *can* reorder items, so unlike a global T it gets a shot at both halves.

```
condition                          better   WORSE   n.s.   (32 cells, 95% paired CI)
ECE   conditional vs global T          10       7     15
AURC  conditional vs raw                6       9     17
```

Still a coin flip on scale, and **net negative on ranking**. The split is systematic, not random:

```
arm x suite                 dECE cond vs global
Qwen3.5-2B-letter  langid        -0.453  better   <- rescues a flattened arm
Qwen3.5-0.8B       injection     -0.154  better   <- rescues an arm below the floor
embed-head         langid        -0.266  better   <- rescues an under-confident head
smart 27B          langid        +0.104  WORSE    <- damages an already-calibrated arm
Qwen3.5-4B         sentiment     +0.076  WORSE
Qwen3.5-4B         langid        +0.073  WORSE
```

Entropy, margin and `label_mass` predict **gross** miscalibration — they detect an arm that has
stopped working — and carry nothing about the fine structure that separates a good arm from a
calibrated one. Which is consistent with Finding 3: the 27B was already about as calibrated as
post-hoc fitting can make it, so there is nothing for a learned map to find.

**The cheap path is closed.** Conditional calibration is not a shortcut to the hard half; it is a
broken-arm detector, which is a useful thing to have and a different thing entirely.

## Finding 11 — two readout formats beat one, but only where both views are competent

We ran `letter` and `name` on three arms, so an ensemble costs one extra forward pass and nothing
else. Averaging the two distributions, AURC, 2000-resample paired CI:

```
arm         suite       AURC name   -> mean    delta [95% CI]
smart 27B   routing        0.1075   0.0830   -0.025 [-0.043,-0.010]  better
smart 27B   injection      0.0202   0.0119   -0.008 [-0.016,-0.003]  better
smart 27B   langid         0.0001   0.0031   +0.003 [+0.001,+0.006]  WORSE
Qwen3.5-4B  routing        0.1623   0.1952   +0.033 [+0.006,+0.060]  WORSE
Qwen3.5-4B  langid         0.0107   0.0454   +0.035 [+0.019,+0.053]  WORSE
```

On the 27B's **worst** suite, one extra forward pass cuts AURC 23% — the cheapest deferral
improvement in this whole experiment, and it needs no training and no labels. On the 4B it is
worse everywhere, because its `letter` view is the one Finding 1 showed is broken: **averaging
with a broken view poisons the signal.** And on a suite already at AURC 0.0001 there is nothing
to win and only noise to add.

Rule: ensemble two views only where both are individually competent, and expect the gain where
AURC is worst.

## What rung 0 decides

- **Rung 1 and 2 are the only path to the hard half.** No post-hoc method — global, conditional,
  or oracle — can reach more than ~0.013 Brier on a working arm. If RLCD transfers, it transfers
  by changing representations, exactly as Finding 7 predicted for the binary case.
- **The prediction stands and is now quantified**, which makes the Jev arm sharper: if Jev beats
  our 27B's *ranking* (AURC) on a held-out task family, that is a real result. If it only beats
  our ECE, it has won a term worth 0.010 Brier.
- **Before any of that, the task set has to widen.** Four suites is four LOSO folds; everything
  above is an effect-direction finding on n=4 tasks, not a transfer claim.

## The capability floor

Qwen3.5-0.8B is below it: injection 0.397 is **worse than always answering "benign" (0.600)**,
only 31% of its raw next-token mass lands on a valid option, and in-domain temperature hits the
grid ceiling (T=20) — i.e. the best fix is to stop trusting it. Consistent with the lab's
standing 4B -> 2B capability cliff note.

## Not established / caveats

- **Jev is not measured.** Everything here is the local side of the comparison.
- Single seed, n=300 per cell. Bootstrap intervals cover **test** sampling noise only;
  temperatures are fitted once on calib and held fixed, so intervals are slightly optimistic.
- CPU latencies (220-340 ms/item for the 2B, under contention) are not representative of GPU
  serving and are not a speed claim.
- The 27B arm reads `top_logprobs` capped at 20; every option landed inside the top 20 on every
  item (`missing=0`), so the cap never bound.
- No `score` primitive yet (the planned Amazon-reviews source needs a dataset script HF no longer
  runs).
- **The cascade assumes the deferred call is free of its own error correlation.** It is not: on
  items the 4B finds hard the 27B is also likelier to be wrong. The simulation measures that
  honestly (it uses the 27B's actual answer on exactly those items) but it does not generalise
  to a *different* fallback model.
- **Pooled accuracy is a macro-average over four suites of equal n.** Those four suites are a
  choice, not a population; the per-suite table is the load-bearing one.
- Deferral rates bought by a calib-fitted threshold drift by up to 7 points from the target
  (Finding 8). Cost envelopes need slack; accuracy does not.
- Sections 3-4 of `deferral.txt` set coverage on the test split; section 5 is the deployable
  threshold-transfer version and is the number to quote.

## What changed under us: open System One replicas (2026-09-20)

Two MIT-licensed open replicas appeared within five days of Jev's launch:
[`GitHub30/OpenJev`](https://github.com/GitHub30/OpenJev) (any HF instruct model, candidate
loglikelihoods, temperature scaling, optional LoRA on a proper scoring rule — no benchmarks
reported) and [`mithalouni/system-one-open`](https://github.com/mithalouni/system-one-open)
(Gemma 3 270M full / Gemma 4 E2B LoRA on Modal; reports 76.7% on a strict subset of TypeSafe's
public eval and 74.8% on held-out task types).

**OpenJev's inference recipe is this experiment's `tiny-grammar` arm**, and its calibration step
is our `T-in`. So Findings 2, 7 and 8 read directly onto it: the temperature step is a no-op for
the ordering of any `noul` decision (Finding 7), does not transfer across task families
(Finding 2), and the thing that actually buys frontier-grade quality is the deferral budget
(Finding 8), which neither repo measures. `system-one-open`'s 74.8% on held-out task types is the
first public number on cross-domain transfer for this class and is the right thing to compare
against once we have a shared eval.

## What would settle the Jev question

One call per item against `typesafe/jev-1.13` on these exact `test` splits, then the same
bootstrap. The single sharpest test: **does Jev's calibration hold on `injection` and `langid`,
the two cells where a transferred temperature fails significantly?** If RLCD transfers where
post-hoc T does not, that is the product. If not, a 4B on CPU plus in-domain T is most of it.
Input-only pricing makes this cents at this dataset size — but it is still a deliberate
exception to local-first, and the text leaves the network.
